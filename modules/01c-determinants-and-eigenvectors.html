Determinants and Eigenvectors

Part B showed a matrix as a move of the plane. Part C asks two questions about any such move. How much does it change areas? That is the determinant. And are there directions it leaves pointing the same way? Those are the eigenvectors, the idea you asked about before the course started.

What you need from earlier parts. A matrix as a move and its columns (Lesson 1.11), the inverse and the number \(ad - bc\) (1.17), rank and the null space (1.18), solving a small system (1.16), and from Module 0 the quadratic formula and factoring (Lesson 0.5).

Lesson 1.21 New

The determinant measures area

Goal: compute the determinant of a 2 × 2 matrix and read it as an area factor with a sign.

In Lesson 1.17 the number \(ad - bc\) decided whether a matrix could be inverted. It was presented as a test with no meaning. It has one: it says how much the move stretches or shrinks area. That meaning is what makes the eigenvalue method in Lesson 1.26 work, and later explains formulas for probability densities and for the volume of data.

  • Keep column 1 fixed and slide column 2 along a line parallel to column 1. Does the area change?
  • Swing column 2 past column 1 to the other side. What happens to the sign and the colour?

The definition

For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\), the determinant is the number $$\det A = ad - bc$$ It is also written \(|A|\). In words: multiply down the main diagonal, multiply down the other diagonal, subtract the second from the first.

  1. Take \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\).
  2. Main diagonal: \(3 \times 2 = 6\).
  3. Other diagonal: \(1 \times 1 = 1\).
  4. Subtract: \(\det A = 6 - 1 = 5\).

What the number means

The unit square has corners (0, 0), (1, 0), (1, 1), (0, 1) and area 1. The matrix sends its two sides, (1, 0) and (0, 1), to the two columns. So the square becomes the parallelogram whose sides are the columns of A.

The area of that parallelogram is \(|\det A|\). Because the move is linear, every shape is scaled by the same factor, not only the unit square. A region of area 7 becomes a region of area \(7 \times |\det A|\).

Why the area is ad − bc

Take columns \((a, c)\) and \((b, d)\) with all four numbers positive and column 2 anticlockwise from column 1, as in the lab's starting position. The parallelogram has corners (0, 0), (a, c), (a + b, c + d), (b, d).

  1. Draw the rectangle that just encloses it. Its far corner is \((a + b,\ c + d)\), so its area is \((a + b)(c + d)\).
  2. Between the rectangle and the parallelogram there are six leftover pieces: two triangles with base a and height c, two triangles with base b and height d, and two small rectangles with sides b and c.
  3. Their areas: the first pair of triangles is \(2 \times \tfrac12 ac = ac\). The second pair is \(2 \times \tfrac12 bd = bd\). The two small rectangles are \(2bc\).
  4. Parallelogram = rectangle − leftovers: \((a + b)(c + d) - ac - bd - 2bc\).
  5. Expand the bracket: \(ac + ad + bc + bd - ac - bd - 2bc\).
  6. Cancel: \(ac\) with \(-ac\), \(bd\) with \(-bd\), and \(bc - 2bc = -bc\). What remains is \(ad - bc\).

Check with the example: columns (3, 1) and (1, 2). Rectangle \(4 \times 3 = 12\). Leftovers: \(ac = 3\), \(bd = 2\), \(2bc = 2\), total 7. Parallelogram: \(12 - 7 = 5\). That matches \(\det A = 5\).

Reading the sign and the size

det AWhat the move does
greater than 1 or less than −1enlarges areas
between −1 and 1shrinks areas
exactly 1 or −1keeps areas the same (rotations, reflections, shears)
negativealso flips the plane over, like turning a page: what was anticlockwise becomes clockwise
0squashes everything onto a line or a point: area 0

The last row is Lesson 1.17 and 1.18 seen from a new side. A move that flattens the plane to zero area has lost a dimension, so its rank has dropped and it cannot be undone. \(\det A = 0\) exactly when A is singular.

The gallery from Lesson 1.12, with determinants: scale by 2 has \(2 \times 2 - 0 = 4\) (areas quadruple). Rotate 90° has \(0 - (-1)(1) = 1\). Reflect has \(1 \times (-1) - 0 = -1\). Shear has \(1 \times 1 - 1 \times 0 = 1\). Flatten has \(1 \times 0 - 0 = 0\).

Solutions

1. \(2 \times 3 - 0 \times 0 = 6\). The unit square becomes a 2 by 3 rectangle.

2. \(1 \times 4 - 2 \times 3 = 4 - 6 = -2\). Areas double, and the plane is flipped over.

3. \(4 \times 1 - 2 \times 2 = 0\). The columns (4, 2) and (2, 1) lie on one line, the plane is squashed flat, and the matrix has no inverse.

4. \(\det = 2 \times 3 - 1 \times 1 = 5\). New area \(3 \times 5 = 15\).

5. \(6k - 6 = 0\), so \(k = 1\). Then column 1 is (1, 3) and column 2 is (2, 6), twice the first.

6. It is 1. Turning a shape does not change its area and does not flip it.

Lesson 1.22 New

Determinant rules and 3 × 3

Goal: use four rules that save calculation, and compute a 3 × 3 determinant by expanding along a row.

You will rarely compute a large determinant by hand; software does that. What you need is to recognise what a determinant tells you and to handle the small ones that appear when finding eigenvalues.

Four rules

Each follows from "the determinant is the area factor".

RuleReason
\(\det(AB) = \det A \times \det B\)Do B (areas times det B), then A (areas times det A). The factors multiply.
\(\det(A^{-1}) = \dfrac{1}{\det A}\)The inverse must undo the area change.
\(\det(A^{\mathsf T}) = \det A\)For 2 × 2: swapping b and c leaves \(ad - bc\) unchanged.
\(\det I = 1\)Doing nothing leaves areas alone.

Check the first rule with the matrices from Lesson 1.13. \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\) has \(\det A = 4 - 6 = -2\). \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) has \(\det B = 0 - 2 = -2\). Their product was \(AB = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 8 & 15 \end{pmatrix}\), with \(\det(AB) = 60 - 56 = 4\). And \((-2) \times (-2) = 4\).

Not a rule: \(\det(A + B)\) is not \(\det A + \det B\). Also, for a 2 × 2 matrix, \(\det(cA) = c^2 \det A\), not \(c \det A\): scaling both directions by c scales area by \(c^2\).

Triangular matrices: multiply the diagonal

A matrix with zeros below the diagonal (or above it) is called triangular. Its determinant is the product of the diagonal entries. For 2 × 2 you can see it directly: \(\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - b \times 0 = ad\). The same holds for any size. Diagonal matrices are a special case.

The 3 × 3 determinant

A 3 × 3 matrix moves 3D space, and its determinant is the factor by which it scales volume. The computing routine reduces it to three 2 × 2 determinants. It is called expanding along the first row.

  1. Take the first entry of row 1. Cross out its row and its column. The four numbers left form a 2 × 2 matrix, called its minor. Multiply the entry by the determinant of its minor.
  2. Take the second entry of row 1. Cross out its row and column, find the determinant of what is left, multiply. This term gets a minus sign.
  3. Take the third entry of row 1. Same again, with a plus sign.
  4. Add the three terms. The signs always go plus, minus, plus.

$$\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{pmatrix} = a\,(ei - fh) - b\,(di - fg) + c\,(dh - eg)$$

Worked example

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

  1. First entry, 2. Cross out row 1 and column 1. Left: \(\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), determinant \(6 - 1 = 5\). Term: \(+2 \times 5 = 10\).
  2. Second entry, 1. Cross out row 1 and column 2. Left: \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), determinant \(2 - 0 = 2\). Term: \(-1 \times 2 = -2\).
  3. Third entry, 0. Whatever its minor is, the term is \(+0 \times (\ldots) = 0\).
  4. Add: \(10 - 2 + 0 = 8\).

\(\det A = 8\): this matrix makes volumes 8 times larger. Zeros in the row save work, so if another row or column has more zeros, software and people expand along that one instead. The first row is enough for this course.

  3. \(\det A = 3\) and \(\det B = -2\). Find \(\det(AB)\) and \(\det(A^{-1})\).
  4. A is 2 × 2 with \(\det A = 2\). Find \(\det(3A)\).
  5. \(\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 0 & 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\). Explain the result by looking at the rows.
  6. If \(\det A = 0\), what is \(\det(AB)\) for any B? What does that say about the combined move?
Solutions

1. First entry 1: minor \(\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), determinant \(3 - 1 = 2\), term \(+2\). Second entry 0: term 0. Third entry 2: minor \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), determinant \(0 - 3 = -3\), term \(+2 \times (-3) = -6\). Total \(2 + 0 - 6 = -4\).

2. It is triangular, so multiply the diagonal: \(2 \times 3 \times (-1) = -6\).

3. \(\det(AB) = 3 \times (-2) = -6\). \(\det(A^{-1}) = \tfrac13\).

4. \(3^2 \times 2 = 18\).

5. First entry 1: \(\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\) gives \(20 - 6 = 14\), term \(+14\). Second entry 2: \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\) gives 10, term \(-20\). Third entry 3: \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) gives 2, term \(+6\). Total \(14 - 20 + 6 = 0\). Row 2 is twice row 1, so the matrix is singular: it flattens space.

6. \(0 \times \det B = 0\). Once one of the moves has flattened space, no later or earlier move can restore the lost dimension.

Lesson 1.23 New

Bases and coordinates

Goal: describe the same vector with different sets of building blocks, and translate between the descriptions.

The numbers in a data row are coordinates relative to the features you happened to measure. PCA replaces those features with better ones and rewrites every data point in the new system. That rewriting is a change of basis. It is also the step that turns a complicated matrix into a simple one in the next lesson.

LAB 1.23 · drag the basis vectors b₁, b₂ and the point v
  • The faint grid is drawn along b₁ and b₂. Follow the thick blue arrow, then the thick orange one. Count the steps along each. Do the counts match the coordinates in the readout?
  • Leave v where it is and move b₁. Does the point v move? Do its coordinates change?
  • Set b₁ = (1, 0) and b₂ = (0, 1). What are the coordinates of v now?

The idea, in steps

  1. The usual coordinates are a recipe. Writing \(v = (3, 3)\) means "3 of (1, 0) plus 3 of (0, 1)". The numbers 3 and 3 are coefficients of a linear combination of the standard basis vectors (Lesson 1.7).
  2. You can use other building blocks. Any two vectors that are not on the same line can reach every point of the plane (Lesson 1.7). Such a pair is called a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\).
  3. The coordinates of v in a basis \(b_1, b_2\) are the coefficients \(c_1, c_2\) that build v from them: \(v = c_1 b_1 + c_2 b_2\). We write them as \([v]_B = (c_1, c_2)\).
  4. The vector itself does not change. Only its description does, as the lab's second experiment shows. It is like giving a location as "3 km east, 3 km north" or as "2 blocks along Main Street, 1 block along the diagonal avenue".

A basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) always has exactly 2 vectors, and a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) has exactly n. They must be linearly independent.

Finding coordinates in a new basis, step by step

Basis: \(b_1 = (2, 1)\), \(b_2 = (-1, 1)\). Find the coordinates of \(v = (3, 3)\).

  1. Write the requirement: \(c_1 (2, 1) + c_2 (-1, 1) = (3, 3)\).
  2. Split into components: \(2c_1 - c_2 = 3\) and \(c_1 + c_2 = 3\).
  3. Add the two equations to remove \(c_2\): \(3c_1 = 6\), so \(c_1 = 2\).
  4. Substitute back: \(2 + c_2 = 3\), so \(c_2 = 1\).
  5. Check: \(2\,(2, 1) + 1\,(-1, 1) = (4, 2) + (-1, 1) = (3, 3)\).

So \([v]_B = (2, 1)\). The same point is (3, 3) in the usual system and (2, 1) in this basis.

The same thing with a matrix

Put the basis vectors side by side as the columns of a matrix: \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). By the column view (Lesson 1.9), \(B\,(c_1, c_2) = c_1 b_1 + c_2 b_2\). So:

From new coordinates to usual: $$v = B\,[v]_B$$ Multiply by B.
From usual coordinates to new: $$[v]_B = B^{-1}\,v$$ Multiply by the inverse of B.

Check the second with the shortcut from Lesson 1.17. \(\det B = 2 \times 1 - (-1) \times 1 = 3\). \(B^{-1} = \tfrac13 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(B^{-1}(3, 3) = \tfrac13 (3 + 3,\ -3 + 6) = \tfrac13 (6, 3) = (2, 1)\). Same answer as the step-by-step method.

B is called the change-of-basis matrix. It exists and is invertible exactly when the two vectors really are a basis, which is when \(\det B \ne 0\).

  2. In the same basis, find the coordinates of \(v = (5, 1)\).
  3. Are (1, 2) and (2, 4) a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)?
  4. In the basis (2, 0), (0, 5), find the coordinates of (6, 10). What does this basis amount to?
  5. For \(b_1 = (1, 0)\), \(b_2 = (1, 1)\), write B, find \(B^{-1}\), and use it to get the coordinates of (4, 3).
  6. How many vectors are in a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)? Of \(\mathbb{R}^{50}\)?
Solutions

1. \(3\,(1, 1) + 1\,(1, -1) = (3, 3) + (1, -1) = (4, 2)\).

2. \(c_1 + c_2 = 5\) and \(c_1 - c_2 = 1\). Adding: \(2c_1 = 6\), so \(c_1 = 3\) and \(c_2 = 2\). Check: \((3, 3) + (2, -2) = (5, 1)\).

3. No. (2, 4) is twice (1, 2), so they lie on one line and cannot reach the rest of the plane.

4. \(2c_1 = 6\) and \(5c_2 = 10\), so (3, 2). This basis keeps the usual directions and only changes the units, like switching from metres to another unit on each axis.

5. \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). \(\det B = 1\). \(B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). \(B^{-1}(4, 3) = (4 - 3,\ 3) = (1, 3)\). Check: \(1\,(1, 0) + 3\,(1, 1) = (4, 3)\).

6. 3, and 50. A basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) has exactly n vectors.

Lesson 1.24 New

The same move in another basis

Goal: rewrite a matrix in a new basis with \(B^{-1}AB\), and see that a good basis can make a matrix simple.

This lesson is the bridge to eigenvectors. A matrix that looks complicated in the usual coordinates may be a plain stretch when described in the right basis. Finding that basis is what the rest of Part C is about, and using it is what Part D is about.

The question

A matrix A describes a move using the usual coordinates: usual coordinates in, usual coordinates out. Suppose you prefer to work in a basis B. What matrix describes the same move, taking B-coordinates in and giving B-coordinates out?

The answer, built in three steps

Start with a vector given in B-coordinates, call it c.

  1. Translate to the usual coordinates. From Lesson 1.23, multiply by B: the vector is \(Bc\).
  2. Apply the move. A works in the usual coordinates: the result is \(A(Bc)\).
  3. Translate the result back to B-coordinates. From Lesson 1.23, multiply by \(B^{-1}\): the result is \(B^{-1}A B c\).

So in the basis B the move is described by the matrix $$D = B^{-1}AB$$ Read it right to left, like every matrix product: translate in, act, translate out. Two matrices related this way are called similar: they are one move seen from two viewpoints.

Worked example

Take \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) and the basis \(b_1 = (1, 1)\), \(b_2 = (1, -1)\), so \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\).

  1. Find \(B^{-1}\). \(\det B = 1 \times (-1) - 1 \times 1 = -2\). Swap the diagonal, negate the others: \(\begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Divide by −2: \(B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & -0.5 \end{pmatrix}\).
  2. Compute \(AB\), column by column (each column of AB is A times a column of B, Lesson 1.13). \(A\,(1, 1) = (3 + 1,\ 1 + 3) = (4, 4)\). \(A\,(1, -1) = (3 - 1,\ 1 - 3) = (2, -2)\). So \(AB = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 4 & -2 \end{pmatrix}\).
  3. Compute \(B^{-1}(AB)\), slot by slot. Row 1, column 1: \(0.5 \times 4 + 0.5 \times 4 = 4\). Row 1, column 2: \(0.5 \times 2 + 0.5 \times (-2) = 0\). Row 2, column 1: \(0.5 \times 4 - 0.5 \times 4 = 0\). Row 2, column 2: \(0.5 \times 2 - 0.5 \times (-2) = 2\).

$$B^{-1}AB = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

What just happened

In the usual coordinates, A mixes the two components. In the basis B it is a diagonal matrix: it stretches by 4 along \(b_1\) and by 2 along \(b_2\), and does nothing else (Lesson 1.12). The move was simple all along; the usual coordinate system was hiding it.

Look at step 2 again, because the reason is there. \(A\,(1, 1) = (4, 4) = 4\,(1, 1)\) and \(A\,(1, -1) = (2, -2) = 2\,(1, -1)\). The matrix sends each basis vector to a multiple of itself. A basis made of such vectors is what makes the matrix diagonal. Vectors with that property are called eigenvectors, and the next lesson is about them.

Order. It is \(B^{-1}AB\), with B on the right, because the vector must be translated into the usual coordinates before A can act on it. \(BAB^{-1}\) is a different matrix.

Practice on paper

  1. In \(B^{-1}AB\), which of the three matrices acts on a vector first?
  2. For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), compute \(B^{-1}AB\). (\(B^{-1}\) was found in Lesson 1.23, practice problem 5.)
  3. For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ 4 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\) and the basis \(b_1 = (1, 2)\), \(b_2 = (1, 1)\), compute \(B^{-1}AB\).
  4. In problem 3, what do \(A\,b_1\) and \(A\,b_2\) equal, as multiples of \(b_1\) and \(b_2\)?
  5. Use the rules of Lesson 1.22 to show that \(\det(B^{-1}AB) = \det A\). Why does that make sense?
Solutions

1. B, the one on the right. Then A, then \(B^{-1}\).

2. \(B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). \(AB\): \(A\,(1, 0) = (2, 0)\) and \(A\,(1, 1) = (2, 3)\), so \(AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(B^{-1}(AB)\): slots \(2 - 0 = 2\); \(2 - 3 = -1\); \(0\); \(3\). Answer \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). Here the usual basis was the good one, and changing basis made the matrix less simple.

3. \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\det B = 1 - 2 = -1\), so \(B^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{-1}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\). \(AB\): \(A\,(1, 2) = (5 - 4,\ 4 - 2) = (1, 2)\) and \(A\,(1, 1) = (3, 3)\), so \(AB = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). \(B^{-1}(AB)\): slots \(-1 + 2 = 1\); \(-3 + 3 = 0\); \(2 - 2 = 0\); \(6 - 3 = 3\). Answer \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\).

4. \(A\,b_1 = (1, 2) = 1 \times b_1\) and \(A\,b_2 = (3, 3) = 3 \times b_2\). The multipliers 1 and 3 are the diagonal entries of the answer to problem 3.

5. \(\det(B^{-1}AB) = \det(B^{-1}) \times \det A \times \det B = \tfrac{1}{\det B} \times \det A \times \det B = \det A\). It makes sense because the area factor belongs to the move, not to the coordinates used to describe it.

Lesson 1.25 New

Eigenvectors: directions that do not turn

Goal: say what an eigenvector and an eigenvalue are, and test whether a given vector is one.

Eigenvectors are the natural axes of a matrix. PCA finds the eigenvectors of a dataset's covariance matrix to locate the directions in which the data varies most. Google's original PageRank is an eigenvector. Whether gradient descent converges depends on eigenvalues. This lesson is the definition all of those rest on.

LAB 1.25 · drag x; the orange arrow is Ax. Hunt for positions where the two arrows line up
  • With the first matrix, drag x slowly in a full circle around the origin. For most positions Ax points somewhere else. Find the positions where Ax lies exactly along x. How many separate lines do you find?
  • On such a line, move x closer to and further from the origin. Does it stay an eigenvector? Does λ change?
  • Only then tick the box to check your lines. Repeat for each matrix in the list. Which one has no such direction at all? Which has only one?

The definition

A matrix usually turns a vector: \(Ax\) points in a different direction from x. For some special vectors it does not. It only stretches, shrinks or flips them along their own line.

A non-zero vector v is an eigenvector of A if $$A\,v = \lambda\,v$$ for some number \(\lambda\) (the Greek letter lambda). That number is the eigenvalue belonging to v. Read the equation as: "moving v with A gives the same result as simply multiplying v by the number λ".

The word comes from German: eigen means "own". These are the matrix's own directions.

Testing a vector, step by step

Is \(v = (1, 1)\) an eigenvector of \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\)?

  1. Compute \(Av\) with the row routine: row 1 gives \(3 + 1 = 4\), row 2 gives \(1 + 3 = 4\). So \(Av = (4, 4)\).
  2. Ask: is the result a multiple of v? Divide matching components: \(4 / 1 = 4\) and \(4 / 1 = 4\). Both ratios are the same number.
  3. Conclude: \(Av = 4v\). Yes, v is an eigenvector, with eigenvalue \(\lambda = 4\).

Is \(u = (1, 0)\) an eigenvector of the same A?

  1. \(Au = (3, 1)\).
  2. Ratios: \(3 / 1 = 3\), but the second component went from 0 to 1, which no multiplication can do.
  3. Not a multiple of u, so u is not an eigenvector. A turns it.

You met both eigenvectors of this A already: Lesson 1.24 found \(A\,(1, 1) = 4\,(1, 1)\) and \(A\,(1, -1) = 2\,(1, -1)\).

What the eigenvalue tells you

Two facts to hold on to

  • Eigenvectors come as whole lines. If v is an eigenvector with eigenvalue λ, so is any non-zero multiple of v, with the same λ. Reason: \(A(c\,v) = c\,(Av) = c\,\lambda v = \lambda\,(c\,v)\). So what matters is the direction, and you may pick any convenient vector on the line, such as (1, 1) instead of (7, 7).
  • The zero vector does not count. \(A\,0 = \lambda\,0\) is true for every λ and tells you nothing, so eigenvectors are required to be non-zero. The eigenvalue is allowed to be 0.

Finding eigenvalues

Goal: find the eigenvalues of any 2 × 2 matrix by solving one quadratic equation.

Hunting by dragging works for a lab. For a real matrix you need a method. It comes from combining three things you already have: the definition, the null space, and the determinant.

From the definition to an equation for λ

We want numbers λ for which \(Av = \lambda v\) has a non-zero solution v. Both v and λ are unknown, so first get rid of v.

  1. Start: \(Av = \lambda v\).
  2. Write the right side with a matrix. Multiplying by the identity changes nothing, so \(\lambda v = \lambda I v\). Now both sides are "a matrix times v": \(Av = \lambda I v\).
  3. Move everything to one side: \(Av - \lambda I v = 0\).
  4. Factor out v: \((A - \lambda I)\,v = 0\).
  5. Read what that says. The matrix \(A - \lambda I\) sends a non-zero vector v to zero. From Lesson 1.18, a matrix that sends a non-zero vector to zero is singular.
  6. Use the test for singular. From Lesson 1.21, a matrix is singular exactly when its determinant is 0. So: $$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$$

This is called the characteristic equation. It contains only λ, and its solutions are the eigenvalues.

What \(A - \lambda I\) looks like. \(\lambda I = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda \end{pmatrix}\), so subtracting it takes λ off each diagonal entry and leaves the others alone: $$A - \lambda I = \begin{pmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda \end{pmatrix}$$

Worked example, step by step

Find the eigenvalues of \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\).

  1. Subtract λ from the diagonal: \(A - \lambda I = \begin{pmatrix} 3 - \lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 3 - \lambda \end{pmatrix}\).
  2. Take the determinant (main diagonal minus other diagonal): \((3 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 1 \times 1\).
  3. Expand: \((3 - \lambda)^2 = 9 - 6\lambda + \lambda^2\). So the determinant is \(\lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 9 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 8\).
  4. Set it to zero: \(\lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 8 = 0\).
  5. Solve the quadratic (Module 0, Lesson 0.5). Factoring: two numbers that multiply to 8 and add to 6 are 2 and 4, so \((\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 4) = 0\).
  6. Eigenvalues: \(\lambda = 2\) and \(\lambda = 4\).

These are the two stretch factors found in Lesson 1.24.

The shortcut: trace and determinant

Do the same steps with letters. \(\det \begin{pmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = (a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc\). Expand the product: \(ad - a\lambda - d\lambda + \lambda^2 - bc\). Group: $$\lambda^2 - (a + d)\,\lambda + (ad - bc) = 0$$ The number \(a + d\), the sum of the diagonal entries, is called the trace of A. The last bracket is \(\det A\). So for every 2 × 2 matrix: $$\lambda^2 - (\text{trace})\,\lambda + \det = 0$$

  1. Compute the trace: add the two diagonal entries.
  2. Compute the determinant: \(ad - bc\).
  3. Write \(\lambda^2 - (\text{trace})\lambda + \det = 0\).
  4. Solve by factoring, or with the quadratic formula \(\lambda = \dfrac{\text{trace} \pm \sqrt{\text{trace}^2 - 4\det}}{2}\).

Example. \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). Trace \(= 4 + 3 = 7\). Determinant \(= 12 - 2 = 10\). Equation: \(\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0\), which factors as \((\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 5) = 0\). Eigenvalues 2 and 5.

A check that costs nothing

For any 2 × 2 matrix with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\): $$\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \text{trace}, \qquad \lambda_1 \times \lambda_2 = \det$$ In the example: \(2 + 5 = 7\) and \(2 \times 5 = 10\). If your eigenvalues fail either test, there is an arithmetic slip. (The reason is the factored form: \((\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2) = \lambda^2 - (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2\), compared term by term with the shortcut.)

The product rule also re-explains Lesson 1.25: A is singular (det = 0) exactly when one of its eigenvalues is 0.

Lesson 1.27 New

Finding eigenvectors

Goal: for each eigenvalue, find its eigenvector by solving one small system, and check it.

Eigenvalues say how much; eigenvectors say in which direction. In PCA the eigenvectors are the new axes and the eigenvalues say how important each axis is. You need both.

The routine

From Lesson 1.26, an eigenvector for λ is a non-zero solution of \((A - \lambda I)\,v = 0\). So for each eigenvalue in turn:

  1. Build \(A - \lambda I\) by subtracting that λ from the two diagonal entries.
  2. Write out \((A - \lambda I)\,v = 0\) as two equations in \(v_1\) and \(v_2\).
  3. Notice the two equations say the same thing. One is a multiple of the other. This always happens, because λ was chosen to make this matrix singular. If they are not multiples, the eigenvalue is wrong.
  4. Use one equation to relate \(v_1\) and \(v_2\). There is a whole line of solutions, as Lesson 1.25 said.
  5. Pick a convenient value for one component, usually \(v_1 = 1\), and read off the other.
  6. Check by computing \(Av\) and confirming it equals \(\lambda v\).

Worked example 1

\(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\), eigenvalues 4 and 2 (Lesson 1.26).

For λ = 4:

  1. \(A - 4I = \begin{pmatrix} 3 - 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 - 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\).
  2. Equations: \(-v_1 + v_2 = 0\) and \(v_1 - v_2 = 0\).
  3. The second is the first multiplied by −1. Same information.
  4. From the first: \(v_2 = v_1\).
  5. Choose \(v_1 = 1\). Then \(v_2 = 1\). Eigenvector (1, 1).
  6. Check: \(A\,(1, 1) = (4, 4) = 4\,(1, 1)\).

For λ = 2:

  1. \(A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\).
  2. Equations: \(v_1 + v_2 = 0\), twice.
  3. So \(v_2 = -v_1\).
  4. Choose \(v_1 = 1\). Then \(v_2 = -1\). Eigenvector (1, −1).
  5. Check: \(A\,(1, -1) = (3 - 1,\ 1 - 3) = (2, -2) = 2\,(1, -1)\).

Worked example 2, from the start

\(A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). Lesson 1.26 gave eigenvalues 2 and 5.

For λ = 5: \(A - 5I = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}\). Equations: \(-v_1 + v_2 = 0\) and \(2v_1 - 2v_2 = 0\) (the second is −2 times the first). So \(v_2 = v_1\): eigenvector (1, 1). Check: \(A\,(1, 1) = (5, 5) = 5\,(1, 1)\).

For λ = 2: \(A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Equation: \(2v_1 + v_2 = 0\), so \(v_2 = -2v_1\). With \(v_1 = 1\): eigenvector (1, −2). Check: \(A\,(1, -2) = (4 - 2,\ 2 - 6) = (2, -4) = 2\,(1, -2)\).

Which vector on the line should I report?

Any non-zero one. (1, −2), (2, −4) and (−0.5, 1) are the same eigen-direction. Two common conventions:

  • Small whole numbers, for hand work.
  • Unit length (Lesson 1.3), which software uses. For (1, 1): length \(\sqrt2\), so the unit eigenvector is \((0.707, 0.707)\). NumPy may also return the opposite sign, \((-0.707, -0.707)\). It is the same direction.

Together, an eigenvalue and its eigenvector are called an eigenpair.

What can happen

Goal: predict from the kind of matrix how many eigen-directions to expect, and know the special case that matters most for data.

Not every matrix has two tidy eigenvectors. Knowing the possibilities stops you being surprised by a calculation, and it explains why data science leans so heavily on one kind of matrix, the symmetric kind, where everything works out.

The four cases for a 2 × 2 matrix

Everything depends on the quantity under the square root in the quadratic formula of Lesson 1.26: \(\text{trace}^2 - 4\det\).

When you see NumPy print eigenvalues such as 0.+1.j, it means the matrix involves a rotation and has no real eigen-direction there.

Reading matrices from the gallery

  • Diagonal matrix. Eigenvalues are the diagonal entries; eigenvectors are the axes. (Lesson 1.27, problem 4.)
  • Triangular matrix. Eigenvalues are still the diagonal entries (Lesson 1.26, problem 5), though the eigenvectors are no longer the axes.
  • Flatten onto a line (a projection). Vectors already on the line are unchanged: \(\lambda = 1\). Vectors perpendicular to it are squashed to zero: \(\lambda = 0\).
  • Reflection. Vectors along the mirror line are unchanged: \(\lambda = 1\). Vectors perpendicular to it are flipped: \(\lambda = -1\).

The case that matters most: symmetric matrices

A matrix is symmetric when it equals its transpose (Lesson 1.14). For a 2 × 2 matrix that means the two off-diagonal entries are equal: \(\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & d \end{pmatrix}\). Symmetric matrices have two guarantees.

  1. The eigenvalues are always real. Check the quantity under the root: \(\text{trace}^2 - 4\det = (a + d)^2 - 4(ad - b^2)\). Expand: \(a^2 + 2ad + d^2 - 4ad + 4b^2 = a^2 - 2ad + d^2 + 4b^2 = (a - d)^2 + 4b^2\). A square plus a square is never negative, so the square root always exists.
  2. Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are perpendicular. Check with the examples you have done: \(\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) has eigenvectors (1, 1) and (1, −1), and \((1, 1) \cdot (1, -1) = 1 - 1 = 0\). \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) has the same pair.

Compare with a matrix that is not symmetric: \(\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) has eigenvectors (1, 1) and (1, −2), and \((1, 1) \cdot (1, -2) = 1 - 2 = -1\). Not perpendicular.

Why this matters for data. The matrices at the centre of statistics and machine learning are symmetric: \(X^{\mathsf T}X\) from Lesson 1.14, and the covariance matrix you will meet in Part D. They have real eigenvalues and admit an orthonormal eigenvector basis, which gives a set of perpendicular unit axes for the data. That is what PCA uses. Part D proves the second guarantee and builds on it.

Bigger matrices

An n × n matrix has a characteristic equation of degree n, so it has at most n eigenvalues. For n = 3 the equation is a cubic; beyond that nobody solves it by hand. Software finds eigenvalues by a different, iterative method. Everything about what eigenvalues and eigenvectors mean carries over unchanged, including: the sum of the eigenvalues is the trace, and the product is the determinant.

The key fact: powers are easy on an eigenvector

Suppose \(Av = \lambda v\). Apply A again:

  1. \(A^2 v\) means \(A(Av)\).
  2. Replace \(Av\) by \(\lambda v\): \(A(\lambda v)\).
  3. A number can be pulled out of a linear move (Lesson 1.11): \(\lambda\,(Av)\).
  4. Replace \(Av\) by \(\lambda v\) again: \(\lambda \cdot \lambda v = \lambda^2 v\).

Repeating the argument: $$A^k v = \lambda^k v$$ Applying the matrix k times to an eigenvector just multiplies it by the eigenvalue k times. No matrix arithmetic is needed.

The method for any starting vector

  1. Find the eigenpairs \((\lambda_1, v_1)\) and \((\lambda_2, v_2)\).
  2. Write the starting vector in the eigenvector basis: find \(c_1, c_2\) with \(x_0 = c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2\). This is Lesson 1.23.
  3. Apply \(A^k\) to each piece separately (the move is linear, so pieces can be handled one at a time): $$A^k x_0 = c_1 \lambda_1^k\, v_1 + c_2 \lambda_2^k\, v_2$$

Worked example: brand switching

Two brands share 300 customers. Each month, brand 1 keeps 90% of its customers and loses 10% to brand 2. Brand 2 keeps 80% and loses 20% to brand 1. If \(x = (\text{brand 1 customers},\ \text{brand 2 customers})\), next month's numbers are \(Ax\) with $$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix}$$ Row 1 says: new brand 1 = 90% of old brand 1 + 20% of old brand 2. Start with \(x_0 = (100, 200)\). Where does this end up?

  1. Eigenvalues. Trace \(= 1.7\). Determinant \(= 0.72 - 0.02 = 0.7\). Equation \(\lambda^2 - 1.7\lambda + 0.7 = 0\), which factors as \((\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 0.7) = 0\). Eigenvalues 1 and 0.7. (Check: \(1 + 0.7 = 1.7\), \(1 \times 0.7 = 0.7\).)
  2. Eigenvector for λ = 1. \(A - I = \begin{pmatrix} -0.1 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & -0.2 \end{pmatrix}\). Equation \(-0.1v_1 + 0.2v_2 = 0\), so \(v_1 = 2v_2\): take \(v_1 = (2, 1)\).
  3. Eigenvector for λ = 0.7. \(A - 0.7I = \begin{pmatrix} 0.2 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.1 \end{pmatrix}\). Equation \(v_1 + v_2 = 0\): take \(v_2 = (1, -1)\).
  4. Write the start in this basis. \(c_1 (2, 1) + c_2 (1, -1) = (100, 200)\) gives \(2c_1 + c_2 = 100\) and \(c_1 - c_2 = 200\). Adding: \(3c_1 = 300\), so \(c_1 = 100\) and \(c_2 = -100\). Check: \(100\,(2, 1) - 100\,(1, -1) = (200 - 100,\ 100 + 100) = (100, 200)\).
  5. After k months: \(x_k = 100 \times 1^k\,(2, 1) - 100 \times 0.7^k\,(1, -1)\).

Since \(1^k = 1\) always, the first piece never changes. Since \(0.7^k\) shrinks toward 0, the second piece dies away.

The market settles at 200 and 100 customers whatever the starting split, because every start has some amount of the \(\lambda = 1\) eigenvector and a part that fades. The settled state is the eigenvector with eigenvalue 1, scaled to the total of 300. It is called the steady state.

The general lesson: the largest eigenvalue wins

In \(c_1 \lambda_1^k v_1 + c_2 \lambda_2^k v_2\), the term with the eigenvalue of larger size grows faster (or shrinks slower) and eventually dominates. So repeated application pulls almost every starting vector toward the direction of the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue, called the dominant eigenvector. If every eigenvalue has size below 1, everything shrinks to zero. If one has size above 1, things grow without limit.

Where this appears

  • PCA (Part D). The eigenvectors of a dataset's covariance matrix are the directions along which the data varies; the eigenvalues say how much variance lies along each. Keeping the eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues keeps most of the information in fewer dimensions.
  • PageRank. A web surfer clicking links at random is the brand-switching example with billions of pages. The long-run share of time on each page is the steady-state eigenvector, with eigenvalue 1. That vector is the ranking.
  • Gradient descent. In Module 0, Lesson 0.9, each step on \((w - 2)^2 + 1\) multiplied the distance to the minimum by \((1 - 2\eta)\), and it converged only when that factor had size below 1. With many weights, the same thing happens along each eigenvector of a matrix describing the curvature of the loss, and the eigenvalues decide which learning rates work. Module 3 covers this.

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[0.9, 0.2], [0.1, 0.8]])
x = np.array([100., 200.])
for month in range(1, 4):
    x = A @ x
    print(month, x)                       # [130. 170.] [151. 149.] [165.7 134.3]

steady = vecs[:, np.argmax(vals)]         # eigenvector for the largest eigenvalue (1)
print(steady / steady.sum() * 300)        # [200. 100.]  scaled to 300 customers

Cheat sheet

Determinant

\(\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc\)
\(|\det A|\) = area factor
negative: plane flipped
zero: singular, space flattened
3 × 3: expand along row 1, signs + − +

Determinant rules

\(\det(AB) = \det A \det B\)
\(\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A\)
\(\det(A^{\mathsf T}) = \det A\)
triangular: product of the diagonal

Basis and coordinates

basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\): n independent vectors
\(v = B\,[v]_B\)
\([v]_B = B^{-1}v\)
same move in basis B: \(B^{-1}AB\)

Eigen definition

\(Av = \lambda v\), with \(v \ne 0\)
v: direction that is not turned
λ: the stretch factor along it
any multiple of v also works

Finding them (2 × 2)

\(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\)
\(\lambda^2 - (\text{trace})\lambda + \det = 0\)
then solve \((A - \lambda I)v = 0\)
check: \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \text{trace}\), \(\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \det\)

Cases

diagonal or triangular: λ on the diagonal
shear: one direction only
rotation: no real eigenvectors
real symmetric: real λ, orthonormal eigenvector basis

Powers

\(A^k v = \lambda^k v\)
\(x_0 = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2\)
\(A^k x_0 = c_1\lambda_1^k v_1 + c_2\lambda_2^k v_2\)
largest \(|\lambda|\) dominates
λ = 1: steady state
