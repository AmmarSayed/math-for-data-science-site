Lesson 1.11 New

A matrix moves space

Goal: read a 2 × 2 matrix as a rule that moves every point of the plane, and build a matrix from a description of the move.

A neural network layer takes a vector in and gives a vector out by multiplying with a matrix. PCA rotates your data to a better viewpoint with a matrix. To understand what those do, you need to see a matrix as an action on space, and not only as a table of numbers.

  • Leave the columns alone and drag x around. The green arrow is Ax. Put x on (1, 0). Where is Ax? Put x on (0, 1).
  • The faint blue lines are the grid after the move. Drag the blue tip. Do the grid lines stay straight? Stay evenly spaced? Does the origin ever move?
  • Put x at (2, 1). In the moved grid, count 2 steps along the blue direction and 1 along the orange. Do you land on the green tip?

The idea, in steps

  1. A matrix is a function for vectors. Give it a vector x, it returns the vector Ax. A function that takes vectors to vectors is called a transformation. For a 2 × 2 matrix, both the input and the output live in the plane, so you can picture the matrix as moving each point of the plane to a new place.
  2. Two special inputs tell you everything. From Lesson 1.9: \(A\,(1, 0)\) is column 1 of A, and \(A\,(0, 1)\) is column 2. The vectors (1, 0) and (0, 1) are the standard basis vectors from Lesson 1.7.
  3. Every other input follows from those two. Any vector is a combination of the basis vectors: \((x_1, x_2) = x_1 (1, 0) + x_2 (0, 1)\). The column view says \(Ax = x_1 (\text{column 1}) + x_2 (\text{column 2})\). So: wherever (1, 0) and (0, 1) land, x lands at the same combination of those landing spots.

That is what the lab's third experiment shows. The matrix carries the whole grid along with the two basis vectors, and every point keeps its "address" in the moved grid.

Worked example: from matrix to move

Take \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Where does \(x = (1, 2)\) go?

  1. Read the columns downward: column 1 is (2, 1), column 2 is (−1, 1). So (1, 0) lands on (2, 1) and (0, 1) lands on (−1, 1).
  2. x is 1 of the first basis vector and 2 of the second, so it lands at \(1 \times (2, 1) + 2 \times (-1, 1)\).
  3. Compute: \((2, 1) + (-2, 2) = (0, 3)\).
  4. Check with the row routine: row 1 gives \(2 \times 1 + (-1) \times 2 = 0\); row 2 gives \(1 \times 1 + 1 \times 2 = 3\). Same answer.

Worked example: from move to matrix

You are told: "this transformation sends (1, 0) to (0, 1) and sends (0, 1) to (−1, 0)". Write its matrix.

  1. Where (1, 0) lands becomes column 1: write (0, 1) downward as the first column.
  2. Where (0, 1) lands becomes column 2: write (−1, 0) downward as the second column.
  3. Result: \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\).

(This one turns everything a quarter turn anticlockwise. You will meet it again in the next lesson.)

What "linear" means

Transformations that come from matrices are called linear transformations. In the lab you saw the signs of one: grid lines stay straight, stay parallel and evenly spaced, and the origin stays put. In symbols, a linear transformation respects the two vector operations:

  • \(A(u + v) = Au + Av\): add then move, or move then add, same result.
  • \(A(c\,u) = c\,(Au)\): scale then move, or move then scale, same result.

Check the first with \(A\) as above, \(u = (1, 1)\), \(v = (2, 0)\). \(Au = (2 - 1,\ 1 + 1) = (1, 2)\). \(Av = (4, 2)\). Sum: (5, 4). The other way: \(u + v = (3, 1)\), and \(A(3, 1) = (6 - 1,\ 3 + 1) = (5, 4)\). Equal.

Not linear: sliding everything sideways, \(T(x) = x + (1, 1)\), moves the origin, so no 2 × 2 matrix can do it. Bending or curving the grid is not linear either.

Solutions

1. (1, 0) lands on (3, 0) and (0, 1) lands on (0, 2). Everything is stretched 3 times sideways and 2 times vertically.

2. Columns are the landing spots: \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \((2, 3) \to 2\,(1, 1) + 3\,(-1, 1) = (2, 2) + (-3, 3) = (-1, 5)\).

3. Row 1: \(0 \times 4 + 1 \times 1 = 1\). Row 2: \(1 \times 4 + 0 \times 1 = 4\). Result (1, 4). It swaps the two components, which mirrors the plane across the diagonal line.

4. \((5, -1) = 5\,(1, 0) - 1\,(0, 1)\), so it lands at \(5\,(2, 0) - 1\,(0, 2) = (10, -2)\).

5. No. It sends the origin (0, 0) to (1, 1), and a linear transformation always keeps the origin fixed, because \(A\,(0, 0) = (0, 0)\) for every matrix.

A gallery of transformations

Goal: recognise the common moves (scale, stretch, reflect, rotate, shear, flatten) from their matrices, and write the matrix for a move.

Complicated matrices are built from these simple moves. In Part D you will see that every matrix is a rotation, then a stretch, then another rotation. Standardising data is a stretch; PCA is a rotation; reducing dimensions is a flatten.

How to write the matrix for any move

One routine covers every case, and it is Lesson 1.11 in two lines:

  1. Ask where (1, 0) ends up. Write that vector as column 1.
  2. Ask where (0, 1) ends up. Write that vector as column 2.

The gallery

Reading each one

  • Scale and stretch. The numbers on the diagonal say how much each axis is stretched. A matrix with numbers only on the diagonal is called a diagonal matrix, and it never mixes the two components.
  • Reflect. A −1 on the diagonal flips that axis.
  • Rotate 90°. Turn (1, 0) a quarter turn anticlockwise and it points straight up: (0, 1). Turn (0, 1) a quarter turn and it points left: (−1, 0). Those are the two columns.
  • Shear. (1, 0) stays where it is, and (0, 1) leans over to (1, 1). The higher a point is, the further it slides sideways, like pushing the top of a deck of cards.
  • Flatten. (0, 1) is sent to (0, 0), so all vertical information is destroyed. Every point drops onto the horizontal axis. This is a projection, Lesson 1.6, done to the whole plane at once.

Rotation by any angle New. Turning by an angle θ anticlockwise sends (1, 0) to \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) and (0, 1) to \((-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\), so the matrix is $$\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$$ You do not need to memorise this now. Check it against the table: at θ = 90°, \(\cos\theta = 0\) and \(\sin\theta = 1\), which gives the rotate-90° matrix.

Solutions

1. (1, 0) goes to (3, 0) and (0, 1) goes to (0, 3): \(\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\).

2. (1, 0) goes to (−1, 0); (0, 1) stays: \(\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\).

3. A half turn sends (1, 0) to (−1, 0) and (0, 1) to (0, −1): \(\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\). Applied to (2, 5): (−2, −5).

4. (1, 0): row 1 gives \(1 + 0 = 1\), row 2 gives 0, so (1, 0), unchanged. (1, 1): row 1 gives \(1 + 2 = 3\), row 2 gives 1, so (3, 1).

5. Both go to (3, 0). Two different inputs give the same output, so the second component cannot be recovered. Information has been lost.

6. First: (0, 1). Second: row 1 gives \(0 \times 0 + (-1) \times 1 = -1\), row 2 gives \(1 \times 0 + 0 \times 1 = 0\), so (−1, 0). Two quarter turns make a half turn.

Lesson 1.13 New

Matrix times matrix

Goal: multiply two matrices with a size check and a row-times-column routine, and say what the product means.

A deep network is one matrix move after another. The formula that fits a regression line contains a matrix times a matrix. This is the operation that lets several steps be combined into one.

What the product is for

Suppose you move a vector x with a matrix B, and then move the result with a matrix A. The final position is \(A(Bx)\). The product \(AB\) is defined as the single matrix that does both moves in one go: $$(AB)\,x = A\,(B\,x)$$ Read \(AB\) from right to left: B acts first, then A. It is the same convention as \(g(f(x))\) in Module 0, where the inside function acts first.

Step 1 of every product: the size check

Write the two shapes next to each other: $$(m \times n)\,(n \times p) \ \to\ m \times p$$ The two inside numbers must be equal. The two outside numbers give the shape of the answer.

ABAB
2 × 22 × 22 × 2
2 × 33 × 22 × 2
3 × 22 × 43 × 4
2 × 32 × 3not allowed: inside numbers 3 and 2 differ

The matrix-vector product from Lesson 1.8 is the special case where B has one column: (m × n)(n × 1) gives m × 1.

Step 2: fill the answer one entry at a time

The rule: the entry in row i and column j of \(AB\) is (row i of A) dotted with (column j of B).

Compute \(AB\) for \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\).

  1. Size check. (2 × 2)(2 × 2): inside numbers match, answer is 2 × 2. Draw an empty 2 × 2 grid with four slots.
  2. Slot row 1, column 1. Row 1 of A is (1, 2). Column 1 of B is (0, 2), read downward. Dot product: \(1 \times 0 + 2 \times 2 = 4\).
  3. Slot row 1, column 2. Row 1 of A is (1, 2). Column 2 of B is (1, 3). Dot product: \(1 \times 1 + 2 \times 3 = 7\).
  4. Slot row 2, column 1. Row 2 of A is (3, 4). Column 1 of B is (0, 2). Dot product: \(3 \times 0 + 4 \times 2 = 8\).
  5. Slot row 2, column 2. Row 2 of A is (3, 4). Column 2 of B is (1, 3). Dot product: \(3 \times 1 + 4 \times 3 = 15\).

$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 8 & 15 \end{pmatrix}$$

Keeping your place. The slot's row number tells you which row of the left matrix to use. The slot's column number tells you which column of the right matrix to use. Rows always come from the left matrix, columns from the right. Use the cover-the-rest habit from Lab 1.8: one row of A and one column of B visible at a time.

Why the rule is what it is

The rule is not arbitrary. It is forced by "B first, then A".

  1. Column 1 of any matrix is where it sends (1, 0) (Lesson 1.11). So column 1 of \(AB\) is where (1, 0) ends up after both moves.
  2. B sends (1, 0) to column 1 of B, which is (0, 2).
  3. Then A moves that vector: \(A\,(0, 2) = (1 \times 0 + 2 \times 2,\ 3 \times 0 + 4 \times 2) = (4, 8)\).
  4. So column 1 of \(AB\) is (4, 8). That matches the answer above.
  5. The same reasoning for (0, 1) gives column 2: \(A\,(1, 3) = (1 + 6,\ 3 + 12) = (7, 15)\).

In short: each column of AB is A times the matching column of B. The row-dot-column rule is that statement written entry by entry.

A non-square example

\(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is 2 × 3 and \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) is 3 × 2.

  1. Size check: (2 × 3)(3 × 2), inside numbers match, answer is 2 × 2.
  2. Row 1, column 1: \((1, 0, 2) \cdot (3, 2, 1) = 3 + 0 + 2 = 5\).
  3. Row 1, column 2: \((1, 0, 2) \cdot (1, 1, 0) = 1 + 0 + 0 = 1\).
  4. Row 2, column 1: \((-1, 3, 1) \cdot (3, 2, 1) = -3 + 6 + 1 = 4\).
  5. Row 2, column 2: \((-1, 3, 1) \cdot (1, 1, 0) = -1 + 3 + 0 = 2\).

$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

Lesson 1.14 New

Order matters, and the transpose

Goal: know which rules of ordinary multiplication survive for matrices and which one fails, and flip a matrix with the transpose.

Almost every formula in machine learning is a chain of matrix products with transposes in it, such as \(X^{\mathsf T}X\). Reading them correctly depends on two habits: never swap the order, and know what the little T does.

LAB 1.14 · the same two moves in both orders
  • Start with A = stretch and B = rotate 90°. Are the blue and orange shapes in the same place?
  • Find a pair where the two orders do agree. (Try two moves of the same kind.)

The rule that fails: you cannot swap the order

For numbers, \(3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3\). For matrices, \(AB\) and \(BA\) are usually different. Take the pair from Lesson 1.13.

\(AB = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 8 & 15 \end{pmatrix}\) (computed there). Now \(BA\), with \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) on the left:

  1. Row 1 of B, column 1 of A: \((0, 1) \cdot (1, 3) = 0 + 3 = 3\).
  2. Row 1 of B, column 2 of A: \((0, 1) \cdot (2, 4) = 0 + 4 = 4\).
  3. Row 2 of B, column 1 of A: \((2, 3) \cdot (1, 3) = 2 + 9 = 11\).
  4. Row 2 of B, column 2 of A: \((2, 3) \cdot (2, 4) = 4 + 12 = 16\).

\(BA = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 11 & 16 \end{pmatrix}\), which is not \(AB\). The picture explains it: turning then stretching is a different journey from stretching then turning. Putting on socks then shoes is not the same as shoes then socks.

The rules that survive

RuleMeaning
\((AB)C = A(BC)\)You may regroup, as long as the left-to-right order stays the same.
\(A(B + C) = AB + AC\)Multiplication distributes over addition.
\(AI = IA = A\)The identity matrix behaves like the number 1.

Adding matrices and multiplying a matrix by a number work entry by entry, exactly as for vectors in Lesson 1.2: \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 11 & 2 \\ 3 & 14 \end{pmatrix}\), and \(2\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \end{pmatrix}\). The two matrices being added must have the same shape.

The transpose

The transpose of a matrix, written \(A^{\mathsf T}\), turns its rows into columns.

  1. Start with \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix}\), shape 2 × 3.
  2. Take row 1, (1, 2, 3), and write it as column 1.
  3. Take row 2, (4, 5, 6), and write it as column 2.
  4. Result: \(A^{\mathsf T} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}\), shape 3 × 2.

The shape flips from m × n to n × m. Doing it twice brings the matrix back: \((A^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T} = A\).

Three facts about the transpose you will use

  • The dot product as a matrix product. Write vectors as columns. Then \(a^{\mathsf T}\) is a row, and \(a^{\mathsf T} b\) is (1 × n)(n × 1), a single number: the dot product \(a \cdot b\). Papers nearly always write the dot product this way.
  • The transpose of a product reverses the order: \((AB)^{\mathsf T} = B^{\mathsf T} A^{\mathsf T}\).
  • Symmetric matrices. A square matrix that equals its own transpose, \(A = A^{\mathsf T}\), is called symmetric. It is a mirror image of itself across the diagonal, such as \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 7 \\ 7 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\).

The matrix \(X^{\mathsf T}X\), step by step

For any data matrix X, the product \(X^{\mathsf T}X\) appears in regression, PCA and covariance. Take \(X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\).

  1. Transpose: \(X^{\mathsf T} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\).
  2. Size check: (2 × 3)(3 × 2) gives 2 × 2.
  3. Row 1 of \(X^{\mathsf T}\), column 1 of X: \((1, 1, 1) \cdot (1, 1, 1) = 3\).
  4. Row 1 of \(X^{\mathsf T}\), column 2 of X: \((1, 1, 1) \cdot (1, 2, 3) = 6\).
  5. Row 2 of \(X^{\mathsf T}\), column 1 of X: \((1, 2, 3) \cdot (1, 1, 1) = 6\).
  6. Row 2 of \(X^{\mathsf T}\), column 2 of X: \((1, 2, 3) \cdot (1, 2, 3) = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14\).

\(X^{\mathsf T}X = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{pmatrix}\). Two things to notice. The rows of \(X^{\mathsf T}\) are the columns of X, so every entry of \(X^{\mathsf T}X\) is the dot product of two columns of X. And the result is square and symmetric, whatever the shape of X. You will use this exact matrix in Lesson 1.19.

From equations to Ax = b, step by step

Take the system

$$\begin{aligned} x + 2y &= 5 \\ 3x - y &= 1 \end{aligned}$$

  1. Collect the coefficients into a matrix. One row per equation, one column per unknown. Row 1 has the coefficients of x and y in the first equation: (1, 2). Row 2: (3, −1). So \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\).
  2. Put the unknowns in a vector: \(\mathbf{x} = (x, y)\).
  3. Put the right-hand sides in a vector: \(b = (5, 1)\).
  4. Check that \(A\mathbf{x} = b\) says the same thing. Row 1 of A dotted with (x, y) is \(1x + 2y\), which must equal 5. Row 2 gives \(3x - 1y\), which must equal 1. Those are the original equations.

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Watch the missing terms. If an equation has no y, its coefficient is 0, and the 0 must be written in the matrix. A term like \(-y\) has coefficient −1.

Two pictures of the same system

Row picture. Each equation is a line in the plane. A solution is a point on both lines: where they cross. Here the lines cross at (1, 2). Check: \(1 + 4 = 5\) and \(3 - 2 = 1\).
Column picture. By Lesson 1.9, \(A\mathbf{x}\) is \(x\,(1, 3) + y\,(2, -1)\). So the system asks: which combination of the columns lands on b = (5, 1)? Answer: \(1\,(1, 3) + 2\,(2, -1) = (1, 3) + (4, -2) = (5, 1)\).

Both pictures give x = 1, y = 2. The row picture is easier to draw; the column picture is the one that extends to data, where "can the columns reach b?" is the question a model faces.

Solving by elimination

Goal: solve a system with two or three unknowns by a fixed routine that always works.

Substitution gets messy beyond two unknowns. Elimination is the method computers actually use to solve \(A\mathbf{x} = b\), including inside np.linalg.solve. Doing it by hand a few times is how you learn what "no solution" and "infinitely many" look like in the numbers.

The idea

Use one equation to remove an unknown from the equations below it. Repeat until the last equation has a single unknown. Solve that one, then work back up.

To save writing, drop the letters and keep only the numbers in an augmented matrix: the coefficient matrix A with b attached as an extra column after a bar.

The three allowed row operations

Each one changes how the system looks without changing its solutions.

  1. Swap two rows. The order of the equations does not matter.
  2. Multiply a row by a non-zero number. \(x + y = 3\) and \(2x + 2y = 6\) have the same solutions.
  3. Add a multiple of one row to another row. If two equations are true, so is their sum. This is the operation that creates zeros.

Two unknowns, step by step

Solve \(x + 2y = 5\) and \(3x - y = 1\), the system from Lesson 1.15.

  1. Write the augmented matrix: \(\left(\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 2 & 5 \\ 3 & -1 & 1 \end{array}\right)\).
  2. Choose the target. We want a 0 where the 3 is, so that row 2 no longer contains x.
  3. Find the multiplier. Row 2 starts with 3 and row 1 starts with 1. \(3 / 1 = 3\), so subtract 3 copies of row 1 from row 2. We write this as R2 ← R2 − 3·R1.
  4. Do it to every entry of row 2, including the one after the bar. \(3 - 3 \times 1 = 0\). \(-1 - 3 \times 2 = -7\). \(1 - 3 \times 5 = -14\). New row 2: \((0, -7 \mid -14)\).
  5. The matrix is now \(\left(\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 2 & 5 \\ 0 & -7 & -14 \end{array}\right)\).
  6. Read row 2 as an equation: \(-7y = -14\), so \(y = 2\).
  7. Go back up to row 1: \(x + 2y = 5\) with \(y = 2\) gives \(x + 4 = 5\), so \(x = 1\).

Solution (1, 2), as before. The last two steps, solving from the bottom row upward, are called back-substitution.

Three unknowns

The same routine, with more zeros to make. Step through it:

$$\begin{aligned} x + y + z &= 6 \\ 2x + 3y + z &= 11 \\ x - y + 2z &= 5 \end{aligned}$$

The order of work for three unknowns: clear the first column below row 1 (two operations), then clear the second column below row 2 (one operation), then back-substitute. The matrix ends up with zeros below the diagonal, which is called triangular form. The first non-zero number in each row, the one you divide by, is called a pivot.

The inverse matrix

Goal: find the inverse of a 2 × 2 matrix, use it to solve a system, and say when no inverse exists.

The formula for the best regression weights contains an inverse. "Can this be inverted?" is the same question as "does the data pin down a single answer?". Knowing what the inverse is, and when it fails, explains a class of errors you will meet in practice, such as "singular matrix".

What an inverse is

A matrix A moves x to Ax. The inverse of A, written \(A^{-1}\), is the matrix that moves every vector back to where it started: $$A^{-1}(A\,x) = x \quad \text{for every } x$$ Doing A and then \(A^{-1}\) is the same as doing nothing, so $$A^{-1}A = I \qquad \text{and also} \qquad A\,A^{-1} = I$$ where I is the identity matrix. It plays the role that \(\tfrac15\) plays for 5: \(\tfrac15 \times 5 = 1\).

Examples from the gallery: the inverse of "stretch sideways by 2" is "shrink sideways by half". The inverse of "rotate 90° anticlockwise" is "rotate 90° clockwise". Only square matrices can have an inverse.

Finding an inverse from first principles

Take \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Use the routine of Lesson 1.11: the columns of \(A^{-1}\) are where \(A^{-1}\) sends (1, 0) and (0, 1).

  1. Column 1 of \(A^{-1}\) is the vector u that A sends to (1, 0), because \(A^{-1}\) must bring (1, 0) back to it. So solve \(A u = (1, 0)\): \(2u_1 + u_2 = 1\) and \(u_1 + u_2 = 0\).
  2. Subtract the second equation from the first: \(u_1 = 1\). Then \(u_2 = -1\). So column 1 is (1, −1).
  3. Column 2 of \(A^{-1}\) is the vector v with \(A v = (0, 1)\): \(2v_1 + v_2 = 0\) and \(v_1 + v_2 = 1\).
  4. Subtract: \(v_1 = -1\). Then \(v_2 = 2\). So column 2 is (−1, 2).
  5. Assemble: \(A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\).
  6. Check by multiplying: \(A A^{-1}\) has slots \(2 - 1 = 1\); \(-2 + 2 = 0\); \(1 - 1 = 0\); \(-1 + 2 = 1\). That is the identity.

The shortcut for 2 × 2 matrices

Doing the steps above with letters instead of numbers gives a formula. For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\): $$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$$ As a routine:

  1. Compute the number \(ad - bc\) (main diagonal product minus the other diagonal product).
  2. If it is 0, stop: there is no inverse.
  3. Swap the two entries on the main diagonal, a and d.
  4. Change the sign of the other two entries, b and c. Do not move them.
  5. Divide every entry by \(ad - bc\).

Why it works. Multiply A by the swapped-and-negated matrix: $$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ad - bc & -ab + ba \\ cd - dc & -cb + da \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ad - bc & 0 \\ 0 & ad - bc \end{pmatrix}$$ The result is \((ad - bc)\) times the identity. Dividing by \(ad - bc\) leaves exactly the identity, which is what an inverse must give.

Worked example with the shortcut

Invert \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\).

  1. \(ad - bc = 4 \times 3 - 2 \times 1 = 12 - 2 = 10\). Not zero, so an inverse exists.
  2. Swap the main diagonal: 4 and 3 trade places.
  3. Negate the others: 2 becomes −2, 1 becomes −1. This gives \(\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\).
  4. Divide by 10: \(A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.3 & -0.2 \\ -0.1 & 0.4 \end{pmatrix}\).

Solving Ax = b with the inverse

Start from \(A\mathbf{x} = b\). Multiply both sides on the left by \(A^{-1}\): \(A^{-1}A\,\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}b\). Since \(A^{-1}A = I\) and \(I\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}\): $$\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\,b$$

With the A above and \(b = (10, 5)\): row 1 gives \(0.3 \times 10 + (-0.2) \times 5 = 3 - 1 = 2\); row 2 gives \(-0.1 \times 10 + 0.4 \times 5 = -1 + 2 = 1\). So \(\mathbf{x} = (2, 1)\). Check in the original system: \(4 \times 2 + 2 \times 1 = 10\) and \(1 \times 2 + 3 \times 1 = 5\).

Side matters. Because order matters for matrices, you must multiply on the same side on both sides of the equation. \(A^{-1}b\) is correct; \(b\,A^{-1}\) is not even the right shape.

When there is no inverse

If \(ad - bc = 0\) the formula would divide by zero. The lab shows what that means: the two columns lie on one line, the grid collapses onto that line, and many inputs land on the same output. A move that merges different points cannot be undone, so no inverse exists. Such a matrix is called singular. One that has an inverse is called invertible.

The number \(ad - bc\) is called the determinant of A. Part C studies it properly; for now it is the test for whether a 2 × 2 matrix can be inverted.

In practice, software solves \(A\mathbf{x} = b\) by elimination and does not compute \(A^{-1}\) first: it is faster and loses less accuracy. The inverse matters as an idea and inside formulas.

Rank: when space collapses

Goal: say what the rank of a matrix is, find it for small matrices, and connect it to solutions and to redundant data.

Rank counts how much independent information a matrix holds. A data matrix whose columns repeat each other has low rank, and models built on it have no single best answer. Dimensionality reduction, in Part D, is the deliberate use of low rank.

The definition

The rank of a matrix is the number of dimensions its outputs fill. Equivalently, it is the number of linearly independent columns (Lesson 1.7): the columns that each add a new direction.

The set of all outputs is the column space from Lesson 1.9. So: rank = the dimension of the column space.

Finding the rank of a small matrix

  1. For a 2 × 2 matrix, compute \(ad - bc\). If it is not 0, the rank is 2.
  2. If \(ad - bc = 0\) but the matrix has at least one non-zero entry, the rank is 1.
  3. For other shapes, ask of each column in turn: is it a combination of the columns before it? Count the columns for which the answer is no.
  4. The rank can never be more than the number of rows or the number of columns, whichever is smaller.

Example: \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\). \(ad - bc = 4 - 4 = 0\), and the matrix is not all zeros, so the rank is 1. The second column is twice the first.

Example: \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). Column 1 is new. Column 2 is not a multiple of column 1, so it is new. Column 3 is \(2\,(1, 0) + 3\,(0, 1)\), a combination of the first two, so it adds nothing. Rank 2, which is also the most a matrix with 2 rows can have.

What collapses, and where it goes

When a 2 × 2 matrix has rank 1, a whole line of inputs is squashed onto the origin. Those inputs, the vectors x with \(A\mathbf{x} = (0, 0)\), form the null space of A. You only need the name and the picture for now.

For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\), try \(\mathbf{x} = (2, -1)\): row 1 gives \(2 - 2 = 0\), row 2 gives \(4 - 4 = 0\). So (2, −1) and all its multiples are sent to zero.

This is why a singular matrix cannot be inverted: if \(A\mathbf{x} = b\), then \(A(\mathbf{x} + \text{anything from the null space})\) is also b. Many inputs share each output.

One fact, five ways to say it

For a square matrix A, these statements are all true together or all false together:

  • A has full rank.
  • The columns of A are linearly independent.
  • A has an inverse.
  • \(A\mathbf{x} = b\) has exactly one solution for every b.
  • The only vector A sends to zero is the zero vector. (For 2 × 2: \(ad - bc \ne 0\).)

When they fail, \(A\mathbf{x} = b\) has either no solution (b is off the column space) or infinitely many (b is on it), as in Lesson 1.15.

Rank in data

Suppose a data matrix has columns "height in cm", "height in m" and "weight". The first column is 100 times the second, so together they count once. The rank is at most 2 although there are 3 columns. One column is redundant, and a regression on all three cannot decide how to split the weight between the two height columns. This situation is called collinearity.

Least squares, first look

Goal: fit a line to three points by hand using \(X^{\mathsf T}X\,w = X^{\mathsf T}y\), and explain where that equation comes from.

This is linear regression, the first real model of machine learning, solved exactly with the tools of Parts A and B. In Module 0 you fitted a line by moving sliders and by gradient descent. Here is the direct route to the same answer. Module 7 returns to it in full.

Step 1: the problem has no exact solution

Data: three points, (1, 1), (2, 3) and (3, 4). Model: \(\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 x\), with intercept \(w_0\) and slope \(w_1\). If the line passed through every point we would need

$$\begin{aligned} w_0 + 1\,w_1 &= 1 \\ w_0 + 2\,w_1 &= 3 \\ w_0 + 3\,w_1 &= 4 \end{aligned}$$

Three equations, two unknowns. The first two give \(w_1 = 2\) and \(w_0 = -1\) (subtract them). But then the third says \(-1 + 6 = 5\), not 4. No line passes through all three points.

Step 2: write it with a matrix

$$X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad w = \begin{pmatrix} w_0 \\ w_1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad y = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$$ The first column of X is all ones, so that it multiplies the intercept \(w_0\). The second column holds the x values. The three equations are \(Xw = y\), and the predictions for any choice of w are \(\hat{y} = Xw\).

Step 3: see why there is no solution, and what to do instead

  1. By the column view (Lesson 1.9), \(Xw\) is a combination of the two columns of X. Those columns are vectors with 3 components, so they live in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).
  2. Two vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) span only a flat plane inside that space. Every possible prediction vector \(Xw\) lies on that plane.
  3. The target y = (1, 3, 4) is not on the plane. That is what "no exact solution" means.
  4. So choose the point on the plane that is closest to y. From Lesson 1.6, the closest point is the projection, and it is the one where the leftover is perpendicular.

The leftover here is the residual vector \(r = y - Xw\): truth minus prediction, one entry per data point. Its squared length is the sum of squared errors, so "closest" means "smallest sum of squared errors". That is where the name least squares comes from.

Step 4: turn "perpendicular" into an equation

  1. The residual must be perpendicular to the plane, which means perpendicular to both columns of X. Perpendicular means dot product zero (Lesson 1.5): \((\text{column 1}) \cdot r = 0\) and \((\text{column 2}) \cdot r = 0\).
  2. The rows of \(X^{\mathsf T}\) are the columns of X (Lesson 1.14). So those two dot products are the two entries of \(X^{\mathsf T} r\). Both conditions together say \(X^{\mathsf T} r = 0\).
  3. Replace r by \(y - Xw\): \(X^{\mathsf T}(y - Xw) = 0\).
  4. Distribute: \(X^{\mathsf T}y - X^{\mathsf T}Xw = 0\).
  5. Move the second term across: $$X^{\mathsf T}X\,w = X^{\mathsf T}y$$

These are called the normal equations. They are a square system: \(X^{\mathsf T}X\) is 2 × 2, so there are two equations for the two unknown weights, and you can solve them with Lesson 1.16 or 1.17.

Step 5: compute

  1. \(X^{\mathsf T}X\). From Lesson 1.14, its entries are dot products of the columns of X: ones with ones \(= 3\); ones with x values \(= 1 + 2 + 3 = 6\); x values with themselves \(= 1 + 4 + 9 = 14\). So \(X^{\mathsf T}X = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{pmatrix}\).
  2. \(X^{\mathsf T}y\). Each column of X dotted with y: ones with y \(= 1 + 3 + 4 = 8\); x values with y \(= 1 \times 1 + 2 \times 3 + 3 \times 4 = 19\). So \(X^{\mathsf T}y = (8, 19)\).
  3. The system: \(3w_0 + 6w_1 = 8\) and \(6w_0 + 14w_1 = 19\).
  4. Eliminate. R2 ← R2 − 2·R1: \(6 - 6 = 0\), \(14 - 12 = 2\), \(19 - 16 = 3\). So \(2w_1 = 3\) and \(w_1 = 1.5\).
  5. Back-substitute: \(3w_0 + 6 \times 1.5 = 8\), so \(3w_0 = -1\) and \(w_0 = -\tfrac13 \approx -0.333\).

The best line is \(\hat{y} = -0.333 + 1.5\,x\).

The formula, and its fine print

If \(X^{\mathsf T}X\) is invertible, multiply both sides of the normal equations on the left by its inverse: $$w = (X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}y$$ This is the closed-form solution of linear regression. It needs \(X^{\mathsf T}X\) to be invertible, which holds exactly when the columns of X are linearly independent (X has full column rank, Lesson 1.18). If two feature columns are multiples of each other, \(X^{\mathsf T}X\) is singular and there is no single best w.

Gradient descent from Module 0 reaches the same w by walking downhill on the squared error. The normal equations jump there directly. For very large datasets the walk is cheaper, which is why both exist.

Solutions

1. \(X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), \(y = (1, 3, 4)\).

2. Ones with ones: 3. Ones with x values: \(0 + 1 + 2 = 3\). x values with themselves: \(0 + 1 + 4 = 5\). So \(X^{\mathsf T}X = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\). Ones with y: 8. x values with y: \(0 + 3 + 8 = 11\). So \(X^{\mathsf T}y = (8, 11)\).

3. \(3w_0 + 3w_1 = 8\) and \(3w_0 + 5w_1 = 11\). R2 ← R2 − R1: \(2w_1 = 3\), so \(w_1 = 1.5\). Then \(3w_0 + 4.5 = 8\), so \(w_0 = 3.5 / 3 \approx 1.167\).

4. Predictions: 1.167, 2.667, 4.167. Residuals: \(1 - 1.167 = -0.167\); \(3 - 2.667 = 0.333\); \(4 - 4.167 = -0.167\). They add up to 0. (The same y values shifted one step to the left give the same slope and a different intercept.)

5. It is three equations in two unknowns, and y is not on the plane spanned by the two columns of X, so no w gives \(Xw = y\) exactly.

6. The columns are linearly dependent, so X does not have full column rank, \(X^{\mathsf T}X\) is singular, and it has no inverse. There is no unique best w.

Cheat sheet

Matrix as a move

column 1 = where (1, 0) lands
column 2 = where (0, 1) lands
\(A\mathbf{x} = x_1\,\text{col}_1 + x_2\,\text{col}_2\)
linear: lines stay straight, origin fixed

Common moves

scale: \(\begin{pmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & s \end{pmatrix}\)
rotate 90°: \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\)
shear: \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\)
flatten: \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\)

Matrix times matrix

\((m \times n)(n \times p) \to m \times p\)
slot (i, j) = row i of A · column j of B
\(AB\): B acts first, then A
\(AB \ne BA\) in general
\((AB)C = A(BC)\), \(AI = A\)

Transpose

rows become columns
\((AB)^{\mathsf T} = B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\)
\(a \cdot b = a^{\mathsf T}b\)
symmetric: \(A = A^{\mathsf T}\)
\(X^{\mathsf T}X\): square, symmetric, dot products of columns

Systems

\(A\mathbf{x} = b\): row per equation, column per unknown
solutions: none, one, or infinitely many
elimination: zeros below the diagonal, then back-substitute
\(0 = 0\): infinitely many; \(0 = 5\): none

Inverse and rank

\(A^{-1}A = I\), \(\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}b\)
\(\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}\)
\(ad - bc = 0\): singular, no inverse
rank = number of independent columns
full rank ⟺ invertible ⟺ one solution

Least squares

\(\hat{y} = Xw\), residual \(r = y - Xw\)
\(X^{\mathsf T}r = 0\)
\(X^{\mathsf T}X\,w = X^{\mathsf T}y\)
\(w = (X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}y\)
