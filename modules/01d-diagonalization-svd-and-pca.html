Lesson 1.31 New

Diagonalization

Goal. Use an eigenvector basis to apply a matrix as two separate stretches.

Warm-up from earlier lessons

Multiply \(\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\\4&-1\end{pmatrix}\) by \(\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}\). Write down the number of entries you expect before you calculate.

Warm-up solution

Three rows give three entries. Cover the other rows while you work on a row: \(2(2)+1(1)=5\), \(0(2)+3(1)=3\), and \(4(2)-1(1)=7\). The result is \((5,3,7)\) written as a column. Review the row-by-row rule if a row went missing.

Suppose a vector is one step along one eigenvector and two steps along another. If the matrix doubles the first direction and triples the second, the new coordinates are two steps and six steps. The lab shows the two pieces before and after the move.

LAB 1.31 · Build a vector from the eigenvectors
  • Set the second slider to 0. Predict the output from the first slider alone.
  • Set both sliders to 1. Compare the two coordinates in the eigenvector basis with the ordinary coordinates on the graph.

Build the factorization

  1. Verify the directions. Let \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}\). Its triangular form gives eigenvalues 2 and 3 (Part C). Row-by-row multiplication gives \(A(1,0)^{\mathsf T}=(2,0)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(A(1,1)^{\mathsf T}=(3,3)^{\mathsf T}\). So \(p_1=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}\) belongs to 2 and \(p_2=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}\) belongs to 3.
  2. Put the directions in columns, and the matching factors on the diagonal. $$P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad D=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$$ \(P(c_1,c_2)^{\mathsf T}=(c_1+c_2,c_2)^{\mathsf T}\). To recover the coefficients from \((x,y)\), read \(c_2=y\), then subtract \(y\) from \(x\) to get \(c_1=x-y\). Thus \(P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\).
  3. Read the product from right to left. \(P^{-1}\) converts ordinary coordinates to eigenvector coordinates. \(D\) multiplies those two coordinates by 2 and 3. \(P\) rebuilds the vector in ordinary coordinates. This gives \(A=PDP^{-1}\), called a diagonalization. Equivalently, \(D=P^{-1}AP\) represents the same move in the eigenvector basis.
  4. Check an input both ways. For \((3,2)\), the three steps give \((1,2)\), then \((2,6)\), then \((8,6)\). Directly, the two rows of \(A\) give \(2(3)+1(2)=8\) and \(0(3)+3(2)=6\).

Why does the recipe work for every input? Multiplying \(A\) by \(P\) gives the columns \(Ap_1=2p_1\) and \(Ap_2=3p_2\). Multiplying \(P\) by \(D\) gives those same columns. From \(AP=PD\), multiply on the right by \(P^{-1}\) to get the factorization.

Check that the basis exists. For an \(n\times n\) matrix you need \(n\) independent eigenvectors in the number system you are working in. The shear \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) only has the horizontal eigenvector direction: \(Av=v\) forces \(y=0\). Two vectors on that same line cannot form an invertible \(P\).

Practice on paper

  1. Use the same \(P,D\) to transform \((0,1)\). Verify by multiplying directly by \(A\).
  2. Compute \(A^3(3,2)^{\mathsf T}\) using the eigenvector basis. Start from \((1,2)\) in that basis.
  3. If you switch the two columns of \(P\), where do 2 and 3 go in \(D\)?
Solutions

1. \(P^{-1}(0,1)\) gives \((-1,1)\). Stretching gives \((-2,3)\). Rebuilding gives \((-2+3,3)=(1,3)\). The direct product reads the second column of \(A\), also \((1,3)\).

2. Each round separately doubles the first coordinate and triples the second. Three rounds give \((2^3\cdot1,3^3\cdot2)=(8,54)\). Rebuild to get \((62,54)\). In matrix form the middle factors \(P^{-1}P=I\) cancel between rounds, yielding \(A^3=PD^3P^{-1}\).

3. Put 3 in the first diagonal slot and 2 in the second. Each slot belongs to the eigenvector in the same column of \(P\).

Python

NumPy uses @ for matrix multiplication. In the composed map, the factor nearest the input determines its first transformation. np.diag builds the diagonal matrix from a list.

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2, 1], [0, 3]])
P = np.array([[1, 1], [0, 1]])
D = np.diag([2, 3])
x = np.array([3, 2])
print(A @ x)                         # [8 6]
print(P @ D @ np.linalg.inv(P) @ x)   # [8. 6.]

Which operation acts first on the input in \(PDP^{-1}x\)?

Lesson 1.32 New

Perpendicular eigenvector axes

Goal. Use dot products to change coordinates for a symmetric matrix.

The two eigenvectors in Lesson 1.31 were tilted toward each other. With perpendicular axes of length 1, each coordinate is just a dot product. Symmetric matrices let us choose a full set of axes like this.

From perpendicular axes to \(Q^{\mathsf T}\)

Orthonormal means perpendicular to one another and each of length 1. If \(q_1,q_2\) are such vectors, write \(x=c_1q_1+c_2q_2\). Dot with \(q_1\). Its dot product with itself is 1 and with \(q_2\) is 0, so \(q_1^{\mathsf T}x=c_1\). Dot with \(q_2\) to get \(c_2\). Putting those dot products in two rows gives \(Q^{\mathsf T}x\), where the columns of \(Q\) are \(q_1,q_2\). For a square \(Q\), these rows undo \(Q\), so \(Q^{-1}=Q^{\mathsf T}\).

Why symmetry gives these axes

A real symmetric matrix satisfies \(S^{\mathsf T}=S\). For example, both off-diagonal entries of \(\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\) are 1.

  1. Take two eigenvectors \(v,w\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda,\mu\). Dot \(v\) with \(Sw\). Since \(Sw=\mu w\), the result is \(\mu(v^{\mathsf T}w)\).
  2. Symmetry lets us write the same dot product as \((Sv)^{\mathsf T}w\), because \((Sv)^{\mathsf T}=v^{\mathsf T}S^{\mathsf T}=v^{\mathsf T}S\). Now \(Sv=\lambda v\), so the same number is \(\lambda(v^{\mathsf T}w)\).
  3. Subtract to get \((\mu-\lambda)(v^{\mathsf T}w)=0\). If the eigenvalues differ, their difference is nonzero, so the dot product is 0. The vectors are perpendicular.

The spectral theorem gives the full guarantee: every real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenvector basis. With a repeated eigenvalue, we can choose perpendicular directions inside its set of eigenvectors. For example, \(2I\) sends every direction to twice itself, so the ordinary perpendicular axes work.

For our \(S\), multiplying \((1,1)\) gives \((3,3)\) and multiplying \((1,-1)\) gives \((1,-1)\). Both input vectors have length \(\sqrt2\), so divide each by \(\sqrt2\). This gives columns \(q_1=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\) and \(q_2=(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\). With \(D=\operatorname{diag}(3,1)\), meaning 3 and 1 on the diagonal, the recipe becomes \(S=QDQ^{\mathsf T}\).

Practice on paper

  1. For \(x=(2,0)^{\mathsf T}\), dot with each \(q_i\), scale each coordinate by its matching eigenvalue, then add the two pieces. Check against \(Sx\).
  2. For \(T=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}\), verify that the same vectors are eigenvectors. What does the negative eigenvalue do?
Solutions

1. Both dot products give \(2/\sqrt2=\sqrt2\). After scaling they are \(3\sqrt2\) and \(\sqrt2\). Rebuild to get \(3(1,1)+(1,-1)=(4,2)\), the first column of \(S\) doubled.

2. On \((1,1)\) the output is \((3,3)\), so the eigenvalue is 3. On \((1,-1)\) the output is \((-1,1)\), so the eigenvalue is −1. That component flips to the opposite side with the same length.

Python

import numpy as np

Q = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]]) / np.sqrt(2)
D = np.diag([3, 1])
print(np.round(Q.T @ Q, 6))   # identity, up to floating-point rounding
print(Q @ D @ Q.T)            # [[2. 1.], [1. 2.]]

What does a row of \(Q^{\mathsf T}\) do to the input?

See the singular value decomposition

Goal. Follow a matrix as a change of input axes, a stretch, and a rebuild on output axes.

An ordinary eigenvector uses the same direction before and after a move. A rectangular matrix can even send two input numbers to three output numbers. To describe stretches for a matrix of any shape, give the input and output their own sets of axes.

LAB 1.33 · Follow one point on the unit circle
  • At 45°, step through the stages. The highlighted input points along the first input axis and ends up on the vertical output axis.
  • Try 315°. Compare the two output lengths. The two lengths you found are the ellipse's two semiaxes, measured from the center to the edge.

What the three factors do

The singular value decomposition (SVD) writes \(A=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\). A singular value \(\sigma_i\) is a nonnegative stretch factor. Capital sigma \(\Sigma\) names the matrix of these factors. Lowercase sigma \(\sigma_i\) names one factor.

  1. Read input coordinates with \(V^{\mathsf T}\). The columns \(v_i\) of \(V\) are perpendicular unit directions in the input space. Each row of \(V^{\mathsf T}\) dots the input with one direction.
  2. Stretch with \(\Sigma\). Multiply coordinate \(i\) by \(\sigma_i\). A zero factor erases that coordinate. In the full SVD of an \(m\times n\) matrix, \(\Sigma\) is \(m\times n\) with \(\min(m,n)\) diagonal slots and zeros everywhere else. Extra output slots, if any, start at zero.
  3. Rebuild with \(U\). The columns \(u_i\) of \(U\) are perpendicular unit directions in the output space. Rebuild the output by adding the stretched coordinates times those directions.

Try a single input axis \(v_i\). The first step gives 1 in slot \(i\) and zeros elsewhere. The second gives \(\sigma_i\) in that slot. The third gives \(Av_i=\sigma_i u_i\) for each paired input and output axis. In the lab, the directions \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\) map along \((0,1)\) and \((-1,0)\) with lengths \(2\sqrt2\) and \(\sqrt2\), respectively.

Practice on paper

  1. A matrix takes three input numbers and returns two. What are the shapes of \(V^{\mathsf T}\), \(\Sigma\), and \(U\) in the full SVD?
  2. For an input with coordinates \((2,-3)\) in the \(V\) basis, use singular values \(4,0\) and \(u_1=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}\) to give the output.
Solutions

1. \(V^{\mathsf T}\) reads three coordinates, so it is \(3\times3\). \(\Sigma\) turns three slots into two, so it is \(2\times3\). \(U\) rebuilds in two dimensions, so it is \(2\times2\).

2. The stretched coordinates are \((4(2),0(-3))=(8,0)\). Rebuild to get \(8u_1=(0,8)^{\mathsf T}\).

Python

np.linalg.svd returns the left matrix, the singular values as a list, and the transpose of the right matrix.

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[-1, 1], [2, 2]])
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
print(np.round(s, 3))                  # [2.828 1.414]
print(np.allclose(U @ np.diag(s) @ Vt, A))   # True

If \(\sigma_i=0\), where does the corresponding input axis \(v_i\) go?

Lesson 1.34 New

Compute a rectangular SVD

Goal. Find both sets of paired directions and their stretch factors for a \(3\times2\) matrix.

The rectangular matrix \(A\) sends input directions into a space with a different number of coordinates. The square matrix \(A^{\mathsf T}A\) brings the result back to the input space, where the eigenvector methods from Part C apply.

Why the stretch is a square root

For a unit input direction \(v\), the squared length of its output is \((Av)^{\mathsf T}(Av)=v^{\mathsf T}(A^{\mathsf T}A)v\). Suppose \(v\) is an eigenvector of \(A^{\mathsf T}A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\). Replace \((A^{\mathsf T}A)v\) with \(\lambda v\). The squared length becomes \(\lambda(v^{\mathsf T}v)=\lambda\), since \(v\) has length 1. Squared lengths are nonnegative. Taking the square root gives the stretch \(\sigma=\sqrt\lambda\). When \(\sigma\) is positive, divide \(Av\) by \(\sigma\) to get the output unit direction \(u\).

Work through the numbers

  1. Form the products of the columns. Let \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\1&-1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\). Each column dotted with itself gives \(4+1+0=5\). Dot the two columns together to get \(4-1+0=3\). Thus \(A^{\mathsf T}A=\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}\).
  2. Find and normalize the input directions. The characteristic equation is \((5-\lambda)^2-9=0\). So \(5-\lambda=3\) or \(5-\lambda=-3\), giving 2 and 8. For 8, the first row of \((A^{\mathsf T}A-8I)v=0\) gives \(-3x+3y=0\), so \(y=x\). For 2 it gives \(3x+3y=0\), so \(y=-x\). Normalize each direction and put the larger eigenvalue first: \(v_1=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\), \(v_2=(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\).
  3. Take square roots. \(\sigma_1=\sqrt8=2\sqrt2\) and \(\sigma_2=\sqrt2\).
  4. Multiply then divide, one direction at a time. The rows give \(Av_1=(4,0,0)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\) and \(Av_2=(0,2,0)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\). Divide by the matching stretch to get \(u_1=(1,0,0)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(u_2=(0,1,0)^{\mathsf T}\). These are perpendicular. In general, images of distinct orthonormal input eigenvectors are perpendicular because for \(i\ne j\), the input dot product is zero. Substituting \((A^{\mathsf T}A)v_j=\lambda_j v_j\) gives $$\begin{aligned}(Av_i)^{\mathsf T}(Av_j) &= v_i^{\mathsf T}(A^{\mathsf T}A)v_j\\ &= \lambda_j v_i^{\mathsf T}v_j = 0.\end{aligned}$$
  5. Assemble and check. For the full SVD, add \(u_3=(0,0,1)^{\mathsf T}\) to complete the output axes. Here \(U=I_3\), and $$\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}2\sqrt2&0\\0&\sqrt2\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad V^{\mathsf T}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.$$ Multiplying \(\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\) doubles the first row of the matrix with 1s, copies the second row, and adds the zero row. That gives \(A\), since multiplying by \(U=I_3\) leaves it as is.

If a stretch is zero. \(Av=0\), so dividing by \(\sigma\) is undefined. To write the full SVD, complete the output axes with perpendicular unit vectors. Their zero-stretch coordinates make no contribution.

Practice on paper

  1. Use the factors for \(A\) to transform \((1,0)^{\mathsf T}\). Check against the first column of \(A\).
  2. Let \(B=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\3&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\). Find \(B^{\mathsf T}B\), both singular values in descending order, and the associated input and output unit directions.
Solutions

1. \(V^{\mathsf T}\) gives \((1,1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\). \(\Sigma\) gives \((2,1,0)^{\mathsf T}\). \(U\) leaves this alone. It is exactly the first column.

2. The columns have squared lengths 9 and 4 and dot product 0. Hence \(B^{\mathsf T}B=\operatorname{diag}(9,4)\), with axes \(v_1=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(v_2=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}\). Take the square roots to get 3 and 2. \(Bv_1=(0,3,0)^{\mathsf T}\) gives \(u_1=(0,1,0)^{\mathsf T}\). \(Bv_2=(2,0,0)^{\mathsf T}\) gives \(u_2=(1,0,0)^{\mathsf T}\).

Python

full_matrices=False asks for the reduced SVD. Here the third output column and matching all-zero row are dropped, so the three factors have shapes \(3\times2\), \(2\times2\), \(2\times2\). A solver can flip both vectors in a pair together, since the two sign flips cancel in their product.

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2, 2], [1, -1], [0, 0]])
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
print(U.shape, np.diag(s).shape, Vt.shape)   # (3, 2) (2, 2) (2, 2)
print(np.allclose(U @ np.diag(s) @ Vt, A))  # True

An eigenvalue of \(A^{\mathsf T}A\) is 16. What is the matching singular value of \(A\)?

Lesson 1.35 New

Keep the largest pieces

Goal. Build a rank-one approximation and measure what was lost.

The SVD describes a matrix as a sum of pieces. Keeping the strongest pieces can give a simpler matrix that still gives similar outputs.

Make one piece at a time

One path through the SVD takes \(x\), reads the single coordinate \(v_i^{\mathsf T}x\), multiplies by \(\sigma_i\), and sends it along \(u_i\). That path is itself a matrix, \(\sigma_i u_i v_i^{\mathsf T}\). The product of a column \(u_i\) and a row \(v_i^{\mathsf T}\) is called an outer product. Each row is the same row \(v_i^{\mathsf T}\) scaled by one entry of \(u_i\). Each output lies along one line, so a nonzero piece has rank 1.

  1. Use \(A\) from 1.34. The first piece uses \(u_1=(1,0,0)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\sigma_1v_1^{\mathsf T}=(2,2)\). Scale that row by 1, then 0, then 0. This yields \(A_1=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\).
  2. The second piece uses \(u_2=(0,1,0)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\sigma_2v_2^{\mathsf T}=(1,-1)\). It has \((1,-1)\) in row 2 and zeros elsewhere. Add both pieces to recover \(A\).
  3. To compare \(A_1\) and \(A\), subtract them, square each entry of the difference, and add. The only nonzero entries are 1 and −1, so the squared Frobenius error is \(1^2+(-1)^2=2\). Taking the square root gives the Frobenius error \(\sqrt2\).

Keeping the first \(k\) pieces gives the truncated SVD. Because the singular values are sorted, these are the \(k\) largest stretches. The resulting matrix has rank at most \(k\). The Eckart–Young theorem says this choice minimizes the Frobenius error among matrices of rank at most \(k\). Its squared error is the sum of the squared singular values you drop. In the example, that is \((\sqrt2)^2=2\).

Practice on paper

  1. For \(\operatorname{diag}(5,2,1)\), write the rank-one approximation and compute its squared error and its error.
  2. How would the squared error change if you kept two pieces? What exactly was restored?
Solutions

1. The axis directions stay in place and their stretches are 5, 2 and 1. Keep only 5 to get \(\operatorname{diag}(5,0,0)\). Squared error is \(2^2+1^2=5\). Error is \(\sqrt5\).

2. The second diagonal entry, 2, is restored in \(\operatorname{diag}(5,2,0)\). Only 1 is dropped, giving squared error 1.

Python

np.outer builds the column-times-row matrix. np.sum adds all the squared entries.

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2, 2], [1, -1], [0, 0]])
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
A1 = s[0] * np.outer(U[:, 0], Vt[0, :])
print(np.round(A1, 6))
print(np.sum((A - A1) ** 2))   # 2.0, up to rounding

The singular values are 6, 3, and 2. If you keep only the first piece, what is the squared error?

Center data and measure spread

Goal. Compute the covariance matrix for a dataset with two features.

Imagine four teams rated on two skills, both on the same scale. To compare how the ratings vary, start at the typical rating on each skill. Then measure how far the teams move from that center and whether the two ratings move together.

LAB 1.36 · Move the data cloud to its mean
  • Notice where team A and the mean sit. Toggle centering. By how much did the coordinates shift?
  • Did the distance between any pair of teams change?

From the ratings to a covariance matrix

  1. Arrange the data. Each row holds one team and each column holds one feature. Here \(n=4\) teams and \(p=2\) features. Both column means are \((8+2+6+4)/4=5\), with the last two ratings reversed in the second column.
  2. Center each column. To center a column, subtract its mean from each entry. A rating of 8 becomes \(8-5=3\), and 2 becomes \(2-5=-3\). The centered columns now sum to 0. Call the centered matrix \(X_c\).
TeamSkill 1Skill 2Centered skill 1Centered skill 2
A8833
B22−3−3
C641−1
D46−11
  1. Compute the variance of one feature. Sample variance measures spread around the mean. Square the centered values, add, and divide by \(n-1\). We use the sample convention here because the mean was estimated from these observations. For skill 1, the squared deviations sum to \(9+9+1+1=20\). Divide by 3 to get \(20/3\). Skill 2 has the same four squared deviations and the same variance.
  2. Compute the covariance of the two features. Sample covariance replaces squares with paired products. Multiply the two deviations in each row, add, and divide by the same \(n-1\). Here that is \((9+9-1-1)/3=16/3\). Positive products come from teams above both means or below both means. Negative products come from teams on opposite sides of the two means.
  3. Assemble the matrix. \(X_c^{\mathsf T}X_c\) holds the dot products of feature columns. Divide every cell by \(n-1\) to get $$C=\frac{X_c^{\mathsf T}X_c}{n-1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}20&16\\16&20\end{pmatrix}.$$ The diagonal contains the variances. The off-diagonal contains the same covariance twice because either order of the dot product gives the same value. With \(p\) features, \(C\) has shape \(p\times p\).

A positive covariance indicates that above-mean values tend to occur together, as do below-mean values. A negative one indicates that above-mean values on one feature tend to go with below-mean values on the other. Covariance is expressed in the product of the two features' units. If you use \(n\) for population variance and covariance, the numerical values scale by the same factor. Here and in 1.37 we use \(n-1\), with at least two rows.

Practice on paper

  1. Add 10 to every value in skill 1. What happens to its mean, the centered column and the covariance?
  2. For the three teams \((1,5),(2,4),(3,3)\), find the means and the sample covariance matrix.
Solutions

1. The mean increases from 5 to 15. Subtracting 15 from the shifted values gives the same centered column, so the covariance stays \(16/3\).

2. The means are 2 and 4. Centered rows are \((-1,1),(0,0),(1,-1)\). The sum of squared entries in each column is 2, and the paired products total \(-1+0-1=-2\). Divide by \(3-1=2\) to get \(C=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}\).

Python

axis=0 takes the mean down the rows, giving one mean per feature. NumPy subtracts that same pair of means from each row.

import numpy as np

X = np.array([[8, 8], [2, 2], [6, 4], [4, 6]])
mean = X.mean(axis=0)
Xc = X - mean
C = Xc.T @ Xc / (X.shape[0] - 1)
print(mean)                  # [5. 5.]
print(np.round(C, 3))        # [[6.667 5.333], [5.333 6.667]]

Lesson 1.37 New

Do principal component analysis by hand

Goal. Replace two feature values with one score, then calculate how much variance that score preserves.

If four teams form a long, narrow cloud, a line through the center can retain most of the differences between teams. Principal component analysis (PCA) chooses the line on which the centered points have the greatest spread.

LAB 1.37 · Turn the projection axis
  • Start at 0°. Turn to 45°. What happens to the spread along the line and to the lengths of the dashed segments?
  • Turn to −45°. Which two teams now project to the center? Compare the variance with the 45° result.

Why the covariance eigenvectors solve it

Pick a unit vector \(v\). The score of a centered row is its dot product with \(v\). The scores column is \(z=X_cv\). Its mean is zero because both input columns have mean zero. Squaring and summing the scores gives \(z^{\mathsf T}z\). So the sample variance of the scores is $$\frac{z^{\mathsf T}z}{n-1}=v^{\mathsf T}\frac{X_c^{\mathsf T}X_c}{n-1}v=v^{\mathsf T}Cv.$$

Use the perpendicular unit eigenvectors \(q_1,q_2\) of \(C\), ordered with \(\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\). Write \(v=a q_1+b q_2\). Because \(v\) is unit and the axes are orthonormal, \(a^2+b^2=1\). Dot \(v\) with \(Cv=a\lambda_1q_1+b\lambda_2q_2\). The cross terms vanish, leaving \(\lambda_1a^2+\lambda_2b^2\). This weighted average is largest at \(\lambda_1\), attained by taking \(v=q_1\). This axis is the first principal direction. The next direction uses the greatest variance available in the perpendicular space.

Compute the scores and reconstruct a rating

  1. Find the axes and their variances. In the covariance matrix from 1.36, multiply by \((1,1)\) to get \((36,36)/3\). So the eigenvalue along \(q_1=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\) is \(36/3=12\). Multiply by \((1,-1)\) to get \((4,-4)/3\). The eigenvalue along \(q_2=(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2\) is \(4/3\). The directions span the plane, so these give both eigenvalues.
  2. Project onto the first axis. For team A the centered row is \((3,3)\). Its score is \(3/\sqrt2+3/\sqrt2=3\sqrt2\). B has \(-3\sqrt2\), and C and D each have 0 because their two deviations cancel in the dot product. The four rows now need only one score each.
  3. Calculate the share of variance retained. Add the two eigenvalues to get total variance, \(12+4/3=40/3\). The first axis holds \(12/(40/3)=0.9\), or 90%. This is the explained variance ratio of the first component. The ratio is defined when the total variance is positive.
  4. Reconstruct from a score. A score is a signed distance along the unit direction. Multiply it by \(q_1\) and add the original mean to return to rating coordinates. For A, \(3\sqrt2\,q_1=(3,3)\); add \((5,5)\) to recover \((8,8)\). C and D both reconstruct as \((5,5)\). Their differences lie along the dropped second axis.

Read the percentage as variance. The 90% describes the share of squared spread captured by the line. Teams C and D still lose their distinction in the one-score representation. Feature scales matter too. Converting the units of only one feature changes its contribution to the variance. For features on very different scales, you may choose to standardize each feature by dividing its centered values by its standard deviation, the square root of the variance, when that deviation is positive.

Where SVD fits

Apply an SVD to \(X_c\), the centered data matrix. We found in 1.34 that its right singular vectors are eigenvectors of \(X_c^{\mathsf T}X_c\) and that its singular values squared are the eigenvalues of that product. Dividing the product by \(n-1\) keeps the directions and divides each eigenvalue by \(n-1\). So the rows of \(V^{\mathsf T}\) are the PCA directions, and the variance along direction \(i\) is \(\sigma_i^2/(n-1)\). Here \(\sigma_1=6\) and \(\sigma_2=2\), yielding 12 and \(4/3\).

For the same centered data, keeping the first \(k\) PCA scores and rebuilding gives the rank-\(k\) truncated SVD of \(X_c\). To get back to original feature coordinates, add the feature means to each reconstructed row.

Practice on paper

  1. What fraction of total variance does the second axis capture? Which teams have nonzero scores on it, and what are their scores?
  2. A fifth team, \((7,5)\), arrives after you have chosen your mean and axis. Using the same mean and axis, find its first score and reconstruct its two ratings.
  3. If you reverse the sign of \(q_1\), how do the scores, reconstructed points and explained variance ratio change?
Solutions

1. The share is \((4/3)/(40/3)=10\%\). C's row \((1,-1)\) gives \(\sqrt2\) and D gives \(-\sqrt2\). A and B have equal deviations on the two skills and score zero on this axis.

2. Center using the original mean: \((7,5)-(5,5)=(2,0)\). Dot with \(q_1\) to get \(\sqrt2\). Multiply by \(q_1\) to get \((1,1)\), then add the mean to get \((6,6)\).

3. All scores change sign. When you multiply each score by the reversed axis, the signs cancel, so the reconstructed points stay where they were. The ratio also stays the same because variance uses squares.

Python

Vt[:1] takes the first row, keeping it as a matrix so one projection per team fits in a single column. The axis from NumPy can have the opposite sign from the hand example, with the corresponding scores also reversed.

import numpy as np

X = np.array([[8, 8], [2, 2], [6, 4], [4, 6]])
mean = X.mean(axis=0)
Xc = X - mean
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)
axes = Vt[:1]
scores = Xc @ axes.T
rebuilt = scores @ axes + mean
variance = s ** 2 / (X.shape[0] - 1)
print(np.round(variance, 3))              # [12.     1.333]
print(variance[0] / variance.sum())       # about 0.9
print(np.round(rebuilt, 3))               # rows (8,8), (2,2), (5,5), (5,5)

Cheat sheet

One eigenvector basis

\(A=PDP^{-1}\)
Eigenvector columns in \(P\), matching eigenvalues in \(D\). For a real symmetric matrix use an orthonormal \(Q\) and \(Q^{\mathsf T}\).

Two sets of axes

\(A=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\)
\(v_i\) comes from \(A^{\mathsf T}A\), \(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}\), and \(u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i\) for \(\sigma_i>0\).

Keep the largest stretches

Keep the first \(k\) terms \(\sigma_i u_i v_i^{\mathsf T}\). Squared Frobenius error is the sum of the squares of the omitted stretches.

PCA

Center columns, then use \(C=X_c^{\mathsf T}X_c/(n-1)\). Project onto the leading unit eigenvectors. Each variance share is its eigenvalue divided by their total across all directions.
